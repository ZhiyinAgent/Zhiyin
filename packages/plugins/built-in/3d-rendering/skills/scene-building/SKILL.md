---
name: scene-building
description: Use when building or changing a 3D scene in Blender: real sizes, placing objects, modelling steps, and checking the work.
---

Work like a careful 3D artist who measures. The person watches each change in the 3D view, so build in steps that make sense to watch.

Start with blender_open_scene: create a new scene file, or open the one the person names. Everything else works on that scene, and it is saved after every change.

Build at real size, with z up: the front looks from -y toward +y, and +x is to the right. Sizes and positions are in metres and angles in degrees; a can is about 0.066 × 0.066 × 0.115 m, a door 0.9 × 2.1 m, a dining table 0.75 m high. An object's location is its origin, as in Blender: a solid is centred on it, and a shape drawn through points keeps the coordinates it was drawn in. A size is along the object's own axes, before it is turned. Planes, text, pictures and drawings lie flat, facing up; rotation [90, 0, 0] stands one up, facing the front. To stand one thing on another, give rest_on ("floor" or the object below) rather than working out heights: it lands on the first surface beneath it, so a cup sits in a saucer's well. Every answer says where each object's bottom and top are and what it goes into; read them before the next step.

Prefer the typed tools to scripts. Add objects with blender_add_objects, shape them with blender_shape_objects (a hole is a boolean difference with a cutter described in the call), and set looks with the material, camera and lighting tools. Wood is a finish on the material, as metal is. A screen that is on, a lit sign or a lamp's shade is a material that glows; with a picture and no glow colour of its own, the picture is what glows. Their approvals read as sentences, and they work across Blender versions. Use blender_run_script only for what they cannot do, and ask blender_api what the installed Blender accepts before writing one: Blender's API changes between versions. A script runs in the workspace folder, so relative paths are the workspace's.

Build things the way they are made, because a picture shows how they are made. Before modelling, recall the real thing: its parts, how they are joined, and the details that make it read as itself, such as a neck, a rim, a seam or a thread. Hollow things are hollow, with walls of their real thickness: a revolve profile that runs up the outside and back down the inside, solidify, or a boolean difference. What a container holds fills its inside up to a level and reaches a fraction of a millimetre into the glass around it and under it: an air gap between them shows through glass as a clear band, and two see-through surfaces in the very same place show as wedges of one and the other. Parts meet as they are joined: a handle's ends sink into the body, a cap sits on the neck it closes, a label lies on the surface it is stuck to. The answers say what goes into what and where every bottom and top is, so a part that floats, sinks or leaves a gap shows there before it shows in a picture.

Name objects for what they are, such as "Lid" or "Left wall", so later steps and the person can refer to them. Attach a part to the object it belongs to (a handle to its mug, a lid to its jar): moving, turning, copying, hiding or deleting the object then does the same to its parts, and positions stay the world's. Lights are named too, and changing one by name changes only what is given.

Check your work. blender_describe_scene gives every object's size, origin, heights, materials and steps with their values, what it goes into, whether a mesh is closed, and what the camera's picture shows, hides behind something or misses; when you can see pictures it also shows the preview. Compare the sizes with what was asked before rendering. A render's answer gives the colour each object shows in the picture, and the background's, so colours are checked without a script. Then judge each picture as the person will: does every object read as the real thing, do parts touch where they should and nowhere else, are the colours and the background what was asked? Fix a flaw a check shows rather than explaining it away: it will be in the final picture too.

Finish with blender_render for pictures and blender_export for model files. EEVEE is quick for checking layout and framing; Cycles is what the final picture should be, and it shows more of what surroundings light and reflect and sees through glass as it is, so check colours, brightness and anything see-through with a small Cycles render first. The camera's exposure brightens or darkens the whole picture, background included: when one surface is too bright, lower the light that falls on it, by its name, or its colour instead. Sunlight is tens of times brighter than the light in a room, so a view outdoors under a sky needs an exposure several stops lower than a view from inside the same scene, and the exposure is set again when the camera moves between the two. A Cycles render's answer says when surfaces are lit past their own colours, which shows them pale or glaring, and gives the exposure that shows them as they are: set that on the camera. The agx tone is the one for sunlight, because it holds bright light; neutral and standard show colours as given under soft studio light and turn what the sun lights into glaring ones. Say where each file is.
